## Summary

Perform a structured refresh of the `execed` short course so it reflects:

- the **completed book** (*The Atomic Human*) as the primary narrative spine,
- the **themes/snippets used in talks since 2024-06-01** as evidence of what has evolved,
- and the **shift in the landscape** (LLMs, coding agents, and human–agent interaction) as a first-class executive concern.

The intent is to keep the course focused on **leaders and decision makers**, but treat the refresh as a **course re-architecture** (not a patch).

## Motivation

The course currently contains strong material, but the narrative and emphasis should reflect:

- the completed arc of *The Atomic Human* (chapters in `the-atomic-human/_chapters/`)
- the increased use of LLMs, software coding agents, and challengs for human-agent interaction
- how the ideas have evolved in recent talks (especially recurring snippet “spines” like *new flow of information*, *human-analogue machines*, *trust*, *uncertainty*)
- the course’s exec-facing tenets (see `tenets/execed/`)

The “business-facing takeaways per chapter” summary indicates the restructuring is deeper than “update references”: it implies a clearer executive storyline:

- **what AI is (and is not)** in organisational terms,
- **where uncertainty lives** and how it should be governed,
- **what trust means** when decisions are scaled and partially automated,
- **how manipulation happens** (System Zero / gaslighting),
- and **how humans and agents co-adapt** (including failure modes and accountability).

This CIP provides a single, traceable plan to update the course deliberately rather than via ad-hoc edits.

### 3-5 business-facing Takeaways Per Chapter

### Prologue
- **The “AI moment” was a branding + investment shift**: once big tech started calling ML “AI”, expectations and capital surged—often ahead of reality.
- **Intelligence isn’t one-dimensional**: “smarter” only makes sense relative to a task and context, not as a universal ranking.
- **Hype distracts from what’s deployed**: today’s systems are largely data + compute + statistics at scale, not human-like minds.
- **Your real business question**: what decisions are you automating, with what data, and who bears the risk when it’s wrong?

### Chapter 1: Gods and Robots
- **Humans are “bandwidth-poor”**: we think fast but communicate slowly; computers communicate insanely fast—this drives who can coordinate and scale.
- **Don’t confuse “an intelligent system” with “an intelligent entity”**: most AI value is better decisions, not robot-people.
- **Beware the “superintelligence” framing**: it’s often the wrong mental model for business risks (which are usually about scale, incentives, and feedback loops).
- **Scaling decisions changes the game**: e.g., supply chains become “machine intelligence” because they make millions of tiny bets continuously.

### Chapter 2: Automatons
- **Large organisations run on “information topography”**: what flows up, what’s filtered out, and where autonomy is devolved determines performance.
- **Automation works best when the task is “information-complete”**: clear objective, defined inputs/outputs; otherwise it becomes brittle.
- **Cryptography is the original data-governance story**: information access determines power; leakage changes outcomes.
- **Production lines for information are powerful—and dangerous**: decomposition (divide-and-conquer) scales throughput but can hide systemic failure.

### Chapter 3: Intent
- **Expertise is mostly “choosing the right context”**: the hard part isn’t rules, it’s knowing which facts matter *now*.
- **Fast shipping beats perfect planning—until it creates “intellectual debt”**: systems get too complex for anyone to understand end-to-end.
- **Engagement-optimised AI creates predictable failure modes**: adversaries can learn the objective and exploit the system (misinformation, manipulation).
- **Resilience comes from human autonomy + common purpose**: “well-meaning chaos” is a feature in human systems; pure automata are easy to game.

### Chapter 4: Persistence
- **Nature optimises for “don’t fail,” not “best”**: evolution produces robust, messy solutions; engineered systems often optimise narrowly and break outside assumptions.
- **Designed systems need guardrails**: if you deploy into the wild (open internet, open markets), assume novel attacks.
- **Beware teleological thinking (“there’s one best way”)**: it leads to bad policy and bad governance (including eugenics-style thinking).
- **Business implication**: prioritize robustness and recovery over “perfect prediction.”

### Chapter 5: Enlightenment
- **Information revolutions reorder society**: printing press → upheaval + progress; computers/social media are the modern analogue.
- **The core AI recipe is Laplace’s**: \( \textbf{model} + \textbf{data} \xrightarrow{\textbf{compute}} \textbf{prediction} \). Most “AI magic” is this plus scale.
- **Control systems beat grand theories in practice**: Watt’s governor is “intelligence” that works because it’s tuned and reactive, not omniscient.
- **Reductionism is useful—and blinding**: great for puzzles, bad for messy human systems and institutions.

### Chapter 6: The Gremlin of Uncertainty
- **Uncertainty is not optional**: it comes from missing data, imperfect models, and limited compute—so “confidence” must be earned, not assumed.
- **Probability is a management tool**: don’t demand certainty; demand calibrated risk and contingency planning.
- **Teams need diversity of decision styles**: planners and improvisers both win—depending on volatility.
- **A key skill is “delayed commitment”**: suspend judgement until you must decide, then act decisively.

### Chapter 7: It’s Not Rocket Science or Brain Surgery
- **Time pressure dictates where intelligence lives**: reflexes for milliseconds, reflection for days/months—design systems that match the timeframe.
- **High-stakes work needs a mission-control model**: clear roles, practiced handoffs, simulation of failures, and rapid escalation paths.
- **“Feel” matters**: humans perform well when tools become extensions of them; bad interfaces create catastrophe.
- **Don’t over-credit heroes**: success is usually a trained, coordinated system—not a lone genius.

### Chapter 8: System Zero
- **System Zero = machine influence below conscious awareness**: platforms learn how to steer your “fast brain” at scale.
- **Immune-system analogy**: centralized “training” + mass deployment without rich feedback leads to overreactions and blind spots.
- **Homogeneity creates single points of failure**: if everyone gets the same ranking algorithm, one exploit scales to society-wide harm.
- **Strategic takeaway**: treat data rights and incentive design as core safety infrastructure, not compliance overhead.

### Chapter 9: Design for a Brain
- **Even geniuses get fooled**: Turing’s telepathy belief illustrates how social cues + weak experiments can mislead.
- **Brains aren’t purely digital or analogue**: real intelligence blends reactive loops with slow reflection and context.
- **The “AI fallacy”**: machines don’t understand us the way we understand each other; they often pattern-match at scale.
- **Product implication**: don’t sell “human-like” guarantees; design for what the system *actually* does and fails at.

### Chapter 10: Gaslighting
- **Paradigms can gaslight communities**: a dominant model (e.g., logic-first AI) can crowd out better perspectives for decades.
- **Surveillance enables manipulation**: states did it with informants; platforms do it with data exhaust and ranking systems.
- **Model blinkers are an organisational risk**: when “the tool” becomes the worldview, you miss what matters.
- **Business takeaway**: invest in pluralism—independent review, red teams, and incentives that reward discovering failure modes.

### Chapter 11: Human–Analogue Machines
- **Modern AI is powered by mega-data + giga-compute**: datasets (e.g., ImageNet) and GPUs made “seeing” and generation practical.
- **Systems can be brilliant and still fragile**: “Sedolian voids” and adversarial inputs create confident errors.
- **Safety failures look boring until they kill**: misclassification + no “pause when unsure” is how you get real-world harm.
- **Do not outsource judgement**: when humans hand hard decisions to “the gods” (or the model), injustice follows.

### Chapter 12: Trust
- **AI is often a “fake plastic plant”**: looks real, delivers value, but lacks the living properties (embodiment, stakes, responsibility).
- **Accountability must stay human**: processes don’t feel consequences; institutions do—so keep humans on the hook for consequential calls.
- **Power comes from data asymmetry**: regulate data collection/usage and align business models, or manipulation wins by default.
- **Treat AI like fire**: hugely enabling, but it needs containment, norms, and competent stewards.

### Epilogue
- **The core threat is institutional**: tech that can’t be controlled + weakened public institutions = “information totalitarianism.”
- **Three practical levers**: regain control of data, build maintainable/explainable systems (reduce technical + intellectual debt), and educate broadly.
- **Shift the centre of gravity away from big tech**: universities, public-sector professions, and civil society must be “the master,” not the apprentice.
- **Measure success by resilience**: diverse coalitions solving “wicked problems,” not a single “general intelligence” story.

### Intended outputs

- A refreshed executive narrative and structure (modules/lectures), starting with `execed/_lamd/the-ai-moment.md` (formerly `the-new-world.md`), that reflects the **completed book framing** and post-2024 evolution (LLMs/agents).
- A mapping from **tenets → learning outcomes → lectures → content/snippets** (so changes remain coherent and checkable).
- A maintainable “content spine” for the course: what ideas anchor it, which snippets support them, and where they live.
- A deliberate place in the course for **human–agent interaction**: when to delegate, how to supervise, and how to measure decision quality under automation.

### Course structure decision (core vs extended)

The book provides a clean “one chapter = one lecture” spine, but the exec course needs to remain outcome-driven and time-efficient.

- **Core exec course (recommended default)**: 6–8 lectures/modules, designed around decisions, governance, and operating model—using *The Atomic Human* as the narrative backbone.
- **Extended series (optional)**: intro + one lecture per chapter (13 sessions), for a deeper seminar format (or pre-read + discussion series).

This CIP will produce a **course structure proposal** that supports both: a tight exec pathway and a chapter-aligned extension.

## Implementation Plan

1. **Lock the “executive learning outcomes”**
   - Turn the existing “3–5 takeaways per chapter” into a single consolidated list of candidate outcomes.
   - Deduplicate and group into a small set of executive themes (e.g. uncertainty, trust, incentives/intent, scaling decisions, System Zero, human–agent interaction).

2. **Inventory the book’s chapter-level themes**
   - Extract chapter titles and 1–2 sentence “what this chapter contributes” for Chapters 1–12 (+ prologue/epilogue as needed).
   - Map each chapter to the consolidated executive outcomes above.

3. **Summarise post-2024 talk themes via snippet usage**
   - Focus on talk collections most relevant to execed: `_ai`, `_atomic-human`, `_business`, `_policy`, `_governance`, `_execed`.
   - Produce a concise map: “Theme → canonical snippet(s) → example talks”.

4. **Review and refresh tenets (in light of chapters)**
   - Validate each tenet against the chapter takeaways (especially uncertainty, trust/accountability, manipulation/System Zero, and human–agent interaction).
   - Decide whether to:
     - edit existing tenets,
     - add missing tenets (e.g. accountability/trust as a first-class executive obligation — see `tenets/execed/automation-with-accountability.md`),
     - or archive/merge tenets that overlap.

5. **Crosswalk: tenets ↔ outcomes ↔ lectures ↔ snippets**
   - For each lecture in `execed/_lamd/lectures.csv` (and any proposed new lecture), identify:
     - which executive outcomes it teaches,
     - which book chapter themes it draws from,
     - which post-2024 snippets best support it,
     - which tenets it operationalises.

6. **Course structure proposal**
   - Decide whether to keep the current lecture list or revise it (add/reorder/split/merge), driven by the outcomes map.
   - Ensure there is an explicit segment on **LLMs + agents** (capability, failure modes, governance, measurement).
   - Produce two “views”: **core exec pathway** and **extended chapter-aligned pathway**.

7. **Update lectures (scope-limited, in order)**
   - Start with `the-ai-moment` (formerly `the-new-world`) to set the framing (book-complete + post-2024 landscape).
   - Consider total renaming of each lecture. Do not be beholden to current course, take guide from book.
   - Then proceed through remaining lectures (or revised list) as needed.

8. **Validation**
   - Rebuild via `execed/_lamd/compile.sh`.
   - Spot-check that lecture landing pages and slides are coherent and that referenced includes resolve.
